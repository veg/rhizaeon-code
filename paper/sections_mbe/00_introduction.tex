\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Homologous recombination—hereafter referred to simply as recombination—drives genetic diversification across most viruses and cellular organisms. Why recombination is so widespread remains debated, though three mechanisms likely dominate. First, it repairs double-stranded DNA breaks in cellular organisms. Second, it purges harmful mutations to reverse maladaptive evolution. Third, it combines distinct beneficial mutations within a single genome to accelerate adaptation \citep{simonloriere2011recombination,didelot2010impact}. Notably, many RNA viruses actively promote homologous recombination even though it plays no role in repairing broken RNA strands, suggesting that its primary adaptive value often lies in shuffling mutations across generations. Recombination rates vary widely across viruses \citep{simonloriere2011recombination} and cellular lifeforms \citep{didelot2010impact,mcvean2004fine}, and breakpoints cluster in specific genomic hotspots \citep{mcvean2004fine,lytras2022exploring}. Depending on where breakpoints fall, recombination can yield offspring that are either more or less fit than their parents \citep{simonloriere2011recombination,lytras2022exploring}. In pathogenic microbes, recombination can alter transmission dynamics, cellular tropism, host range, pathogenicity, antigenicity, immune evasion, and antimicrobial resistance \citep{alfonsi2024data,didelot2010impact,croucher2011rapid}. In sexually reproducing eukaryotes, meiotic recombination purges deleterious mutations across generations and assembles hybrid genomes with novel phenotypes \citep{rieseberg2003major,mcvean2004fine}.

Beyond its biological role, recombination confounds phylogenetic analyses. Standard phylogenetics assumes that homologous sequences share a single bifurcating tree. When a recombinant genome combines sequence tracts from divergent parents, forcing it onto a single tree shortens deep internal branches, lengthens terminal branches, and creates spurious clades \citep{schierup2000consequences,kosakovsky2006gard}. These distortions bias molecular clock estimates, node dating, ancestral sequence reconstruction, transmission cluster inference, and demographic history \citep{schierup2000consequences,posada2002evaluation}. In codon-based selection analyses, recombination-induced homoplasies mimic convergent amino acid substitutions across branches, causing standard tests such as MEME and BUSTED \citep{murrell2012detecting,murrell2015gene} to generate false-positive signals of diversifying selection \citep{kosakovsky2006gard}.

Consequently, molecular evolution pipelines routinely screen alignments to locate recombination breakpoints and identify recombinant sequences. Whole-alignment partitioning methods such as GARD \citep{kosakovsky2006gard} locate disruptive breakpoints across an alignment and split it into non-recombinant segments for downstream phylogenetic inference. When breakpoints are so dense that partitioned segments become too short for reliable tree inference, ``triplet-scanning'' methods such as 3SEQ \citep{boni2007exact,lam20183seq} and RDP5 \citep{martin2021rdp5} test every three-sequence combination to localize breakpoints within individual genomes, allowing researchers to mask or remove recombined tracts. These two strategies reflect a deeper algorithmic divide. GARD formulates breakpoint detection as a phylogenetic model selection problem under continuous-time Markov substitution models, using likelihood ratio tests to optimize breakpoint positions and segment trees. Because the search space of partition boundaries and tree topologies scales exponentially with the number of breakpoints $B$ and cubically with the number of taxa $N$ ($\mathcal{O}(L^B \cdot N^3)$ for alignment length $L$), multi-breakpoint GARD searches across hundreds of genomes require days of cluster computing.

In contrast, triplet-scanning methods such as 3SEQ and RDP5 avoid expensive phylogenetic likelihood optimizations and instead scan sequence triplets for localized shifts in pairwise sequence similarity. Although fast on alignments of a few hundred sequences, exhaustive triplet scanners scale cubically with taxon count ($\mathcal{O}(N^3 L)$) and become impractical above a few thousand genomes. Moreover, because an isolated sequence triplet lacks a broader phylogenetic reference frame, triplet scanners cannot inherently distinguish the recombinant sequence from its parents—especially when close relatives of the parents are unsampled. Triplet scanners therefore require downstream phylogenetic post-processing to assign parent and recombinant roles and to consolidate redundant calls. Without consolidation, a single ancestral recombination event can trigger tens or hundreds of separate triplet alerts. Finally, without phylogenetic context, triplet scanners can mistake lineage-specific bursts of substitution in a genomic region (locus- or site-specific heterotachy) for recombination. Adding these downstream phylogenetic checks substantially increases total runtime beyond the initial triplet scan. Here we introduce \RhizAeon, a fast, open-source recombination detection framework that scales linearly with dataset size while preserving phylogenetic context without building discrete trees. Its name combines the Greek \textit{rhiza} (root) and \textit{aeon} (deep evolutionary time), evoking the interconnected biological rhizome in place of strictly bifurcating trees.
